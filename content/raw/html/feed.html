The 18th century Unitarian Meeting House, certainly one of Ipswich’s most significant buildings, has been a place of worship for 320 years, since opening its doors in 1700. On the day work began on the restoration, Historic England confirmed it would be making a grant for £421,000 for the work. The church had already raised £20,000 of its own, as well as been awarded further grants, boosting the funds to £125,000.

Historic England has been heavily involved in the development of the plans for the project, giving expert fundraising advice, and technical guidance.

The Unitarian Meeting House had been placed on the Heritage at Risk Register due to the poor condition of the building, from which it will be removed once repairs are completed.

]]> There are some incredible buildings dotted around the UK, both old and new, and it’s important from a historical perspective that these structures are preserved for future generations… and this is why we have listed building status, to ensure the protection of any sites of particular architectural or historic interest.

The older the building in question, the more likely it is to achieve listed status. Generally speaking, according to Historic England (which is in charge of looking after sites all over the country), any buildings built before 1700 and which are close to their original condition will most likely be listed.

As are the majority of buildings built between 1700 and 1850. It’s after 1945 that more careful selection is required and sites that are under 30 years old wouldn’t usually be viewed as having special historic or architectural interest because they haven’t stood the test of time.

There are grade I and grade II listed buildings, with grade I sites of exceptional interest. Just 2.5 per cent of all listed buildings are classified as grade I, while grade II* sites are important buildings of more than special interest (5.8 per cent of all listed buildings). Grade II buildings, meanwhile, are those of special interest, making up 91.7 per cent of all listed buildings.

Homeowners are most likely to find themselves living in grade II listed buildings and it’s important that you know your responsibilities where the structure is concerned, particularly when it comes to making changes.

Listed buildings are afforded extra legal protection within the planning system because of their importance to the nation and you will likely find that there is more control over what changes you can make to both the interior and exterior of the building – which is something to think about before you purchase a home of this kind.

If any work you have in mind will affect the special architectural or historic interest of the house, you will need to apply for listed building consent. Get in touch with your local authority conservation officer to see if consent will be required for what you have in mind.

When applications are considered, your planning authority will look in particular at the desirability of preserving the building, as well as its features and setting – and you would be wise to think about this when planning any changes you want to make.

Also bear in mind at all times that any unauthorised work you carry out on site will be considered a criminal offence and you could be prosecuted as a result. Planning authorities can also insist that any work done without consent in place be reversed, which could prove very expensive.

One of the most time-consuming parts of an old house to restore is the flooring.

Carpets, lino and veneer flooring can work well in different settings, but if you already have wood floors it is worth considering restoring them.

Wood floors are attractive, hygienic and don’t harbour dust mites. They also don’t necessarily cost more to restore than buying new flooring.

So why might you need your parquet floor restored?

  1. There are loose tiles

It is a good idea to get these seen to asap as they can present a hazard, and may get damaged.

  1. It needs sanding

Scratches over time mean that a floor will eventually need resanding.

  1. It needs polishing

While some people prefer oils and waxes, lacquer is often considered a better bet for a hardwearing finish.

  1. It has been damaged

Whether it is from scratches, incorrect care or just age, a floor restoration service can bring it up like new.

  1. There are gaps inbetween tiles

This can be remedied with a specific technique that involves using the sawdust from sanding to fill in gaps.

If you do decide to have your parquet floor restored then you need to consider bringing in the professionals. Laying parquet tiles is a tricky and time-consuming job.

More importantly, it is important to not attempt to sand your floors yourself using a standard drum sander. This is as parquet floors have the grain running in different directions and a drum sander doesn’t handle this well. You need a specific type of sander to deal with delicate parquet flooring.
